Socrates

Ancient Athens' original philosophical provocateur, famous for dialectic inquiry, moral self-examination, and a life devoted to wisdom.

Era: 5th century BCE • Main themes: Ethics, Knowledge, Dialogue

Introduction

Often regarded as the founding father of Western philosophy, Socrates is a unique historical figure because he never wrote down a single word of his own teachings. Everything we know about him comes from the accounts of his students, most notably Plato and Xenophon. Socrates spent his days in the marketplaces of Athens, engaging citizens in relentless dialogues.

Core Philosophy

Socrates believed that true wisdom begins with recognizing one's own ignorance. He developed what is now known as the "Socratic Method"—a system of inquiry that involves asking a series of probing questions to expose contradictions in someone's beliefs and help them arrive at a deeper truth.

Practical Impact

He believed that philosophy should achieve practical results for the greater well-being of society, arguing that true knowledge naturally leads to moral goodness.

Impact

His dedication to questioning authority and examining life made him enemies. He was ultimately put on trial and sentenced to death by drinking poison hemlock for "corrupting the youth" and failing to acknowledge the city's official gods. His willingness to die for his principles made him a philosophical martyr.

Notable Quotes

“The unexamined life is not worth living.”

“I know that I know nothing.”
